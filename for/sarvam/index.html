A field note for Sarvam: Deployment Strategist and Agent Engineer

A Hindi farm helpline agent, tested on 200 real calls

Naivedhya Agrawal · New Delhi · 10 November 2026

The Kisan Call Centre answers farmers' questions by phone, and the Ministry of Agriculture publishes its call log. For Uttar Pradesh in 2024 that is 100,000 calls from 79 districts, each with the advisor's note of the question and the answer given; 99.5% of the answers are in Hindi.

I built a proof of concept of the agent such a helpline would put on its line, on Sarvam's own stack: Sarvam-105B for the conversation and the tool calls, Bulbul for the voice. Then I tested it on 200 real calls from September to November 2024, next to the same model with no tools, and had Sarvam-105B grade both against what the human advisor actually said.

Hear three of its answers, or read all 200 calls, the agent's spec and the test harness on GitHub.

every call is real; nothing here is made up

What farmers actually ask

Read by the advisors' own notes, 44% of the calls ask about the weather, 25% about government schemes, most of them PM-KISAN payments, and 22% about pests, diseases and fertilizer.

The category field tells a different story on 13.7% of calls. A team that sizes its agent from that field plans for 31% weather traffic; the notes say 44%. So the first job on any deployment is to read the conversations, not the dashboard.

Three kinds of question, three sources of truth

Each kind of question needs its facts from somewhere other than the model's memory, and a safe move when the facts are missing.

the model routes; the facts come from tools

QuestionWhere the answer comes fromWhen there is no answer
Weather A forecast tool for the caller's district. In this test it returns the weather models' forecast for that district and the next five days from the Open-Meteo archive. The agent may say only the numbers it returns, and adds one tip for the crop. No forecast, no numbers: the call goes to an expert.
Schemes A dated fact card built from the advisors' own answers: how to check PM-KISAN status, e-KYC, the helplines, the 72-hour rule for crop loss, and which instalment had been released on the day of the call. The agent never asks for an Aadhaar number, a bank account or an OTP. The official route to check, and the helpline.
Pests, diseases, fertilizer The 21,758 answers the advisors gave between January and August 2024. The agent searches them and picks one by its id; the system then reads that answer to the caller word for word, with a fixed safety line for sprays. No past answer for the same crop and problem: the call goes to a human expert.

What testing caught

Before the real test I ran the agent on six calls from July and August, the months the answer bank comes from. One of them, a guava call, went wrong twice, in two ways that matter on a farm, and both fixes went into code rather than into the prompt. Two more catches came after the real test, and both were my bugs, not the model's.

a prompt rule is a request; code is a guarantee

  1. 1A whitefly question got a fruit-fly spray

    A farmer in Moradabad asked about whitefly on guava. The search matched the word "fly", and the agent passed on a quinalphos spray meant for fruit fly. The prompt already said to use only an answer for the same problem. The fix moved that rule into the search: a past answer comes back only if its question covers the caller's crop and problem.

  2. 2A verified dose was read out double

    The verified answer said imidacloprid 17.8% SL at 1 ml per 2 litres of water. The agent told the farmer 1 ml per litre. Now the model never says a dose in its own words: it picks a verified answer by its id, and the system reads that answer out as written. A number guard checks every other number against the tool results before the voice speaks.

  3. 3My own guard misread Hindi numbers

    On this laptop's Windows set-up, R's punctuation class also matched Hindi vowel signs, so छह हजार (six thousand) was read as a plain 6 and most number words were not read at all. The guard sent 16 answers to an expert, 15 of them about schemes, and let 4 weather answers through unchecked. I fixed the parser, changed nothing else, and re-ran the same 200 calls from the cached model answers. 14 of the 16 went through, and the 4 weather answers were stopped. One of them had promised rain above 80% today and tomorrow, when the forecast for tomorrow said 8%.

  4. 4My grader could not see the block

    The grader saw each call's date, district and crop but not the block, which both answers had been given, so when the plain model named the caller's block it was marked as made up. I gave the grader the same context and re-graded every call. The plain model's answers with no source fell from 48% to 25%, and this page uses the new grades. The re-grade also tested the grader: 27% of its grades moved, and the agent's score went from 62% to 55%. The agent's lead held under both graders, so read the gap, not the exact level.

The test: 200 calls from September to November

The agent gave the same or partly the same advice as the advisor on 55% of calls, against 36% for the same model with no tools. Paired call by call, the 95% bootstrap interval for the agent's lead runs from 10 to 28 points.

On weather calls the agent called the forecast tool 98% of the time, and its advice was the same or partly the same as the advisor's on 85% of them. The same model with no tools has no forecast, yet 39 of its 40 weather answers told the farmer what the weather would do. 30 promised clear or dry weather, as in मौसम साफ रहेगा (the weather will stay clear), and for 22 of those the forecast for that district gave rain a 50% or higher chance that day or the next.

On pest, disease and fertilizer calls the agent read a verified answer 78% of the time and sent 22% to an expert, and it never put a dose in its own words. The model with no tools gave a dose or a product formulation on 12% of those calls, and the grader found a claim with no source in 38% of its answers.

In 9 answers the model with no tools gave a phone number that appears in no official list and not in the advisor's answer; the agent did that in none. 26% of the agent's calls went to a human. Most of them point at a gap to close: a missing verified answer, a price feed, or a fact the card lacks. 8 came from the number guard: 4 weather answers with a number the forecast did not give, and 4 right answers my parser still misreads, such as दो-दो हजार (two thousand each). The parser cases are the next fix, to be checked on fresh calls rather than these 200.

Fast enough for a phone line

Half the calls needed 422 ms or less of model time, all turns added up, and 95% finished within 673 ms, over 1.9 model turns on average. I measured this from a laptop in Jaipur, so it includes the network.

An answer used about 2,396 input and 66 output tokens on average, which is Rs 40 per 1,000 answers for the model and about Rs 406 per 1,000 answers for Bulbul's voice at the standard price. Most of the input is the spec and the tool definitions, which is the part to cache first.

What I would tell the programme head

  1. 1Size the agent from the conversations

    The category field undercounts weather by 13 points. The notes and the answers are the real specification of what the agent must handle, and reading them takes a day.

  2. 2Doses are read, never generated

    For anything a farmer will spray, the agent chooses and the system speaks a verified answer word for word. The answer bank, kept by the subject matter experts, becomes the product.

  3. 3Treat a handoff as a measured outcome

    A handoff is the right answer when the bank has nothing. Track the rate by question type each week; each one is a question the experts answer once, and it goes into the bank.

  4. 4Keep the test calls as the regression suite

    The 200 calls and their grades are the gate for every change of prompt, tool or model, and the dev failures above stay in the suite for good. The grader needs the same care: the experts grade a sample of calls by hand each month, because one changed line of context moved 27% of mine.

The first 90 days with a state helpline

Written for a state agriculture department or a central programme taking a helpline like this from pilot to production.

the KPIs are agreed on day one, never after

DaysWhat happensWhat gets measured
1 to 15 Read a month of real calls with the advisors and map the questions the way this note does. Agree the KPIs with the programme head and the data rules with the department: what is stored, for how long, and who reviews it. Load the answer bank and the scheme facts, each with an owner. A baseline from the current line: calls answered, time to answer, repeat calls, and how often advisors refer callers elsewhere.
16 to 45 Two or three districts, with the agent in shadow mode next to the advisors: Saaras for the caller's speech, Sarvam-105B for routing and tools, Bulbul for the voice. Every dose answer is reviewed by an expert. Weekly evals on the past week's calls. Same advice as the advisor, answers with no source (target zero), handoff rate by question type, and model time per call.
46 to 90 Go live in the pilot districts with a human one transfer away, then widen district by district. Close the top gaps in the answer bank every week. A readout to the programme head with the KPIs against the baseline. The agreed KPIs against the baseline, cost per answered call, and the plan for the next quarter.

How this was made. Data: the Kisan Call Centre query log for Uttar Pradesh, 2024, published by the Ministry of Agriculture & Farmers Welfare on data.gov.in under the Government Open Data Licence, India, and mirrored on Hugging Face (rishabha25/kcc_up_2024_100k). Each call's question type was read from the advisor's note, with the category field second. The answer bank is the 79,348 calls from January to August; the 200 test calls are distinct questions from September to November, sampled by question type. Weather facts: the Open-Meteo historical forecast archive for each district, from the call date to four days after it. The agent is sarvam-105b-conversations with tool calling and thinking off; the comparison is the same model with the same context and no tools; the grader is sarvam-105b with a fixed rubric that sees the same call context as the answers, run on both. Phone-number, dose and forecast checks are computed in code. This is my own proof of concept, not a Kisan Call Centre or government system, and no real caller heard it.
