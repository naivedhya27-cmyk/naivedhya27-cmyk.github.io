A field note for SpaceXSI: AI Deployment Manager and Forward Deployed Engineer, India

Five weeks of agent work, measured

Naivedhya Agrawal · New Delhi · 10 November 2026

From 3 October to 7 November 2026, our two-person company ran most of its delivery through an AI coding agent: research protocols, statistics, systematic reviews, client files and the software behind them. Every number here was counted from the agent's own session logs on our laptop, by a script that writes out counts and nothing else.

It is the view I would want of any team I roll an AI coding tool out to, so I have written it the way I would for a CTO: what was measured, what it says, and what I would do in the first 90 days at an Indian engineering organisation.

A working day

On the median day a person gave the agent 96 instructions; the busiest day, 13 October, had 186. An instruction is a short message in plain language, typed or dictated. The agent carries it out with the tools it has on the laptop: files, a shell, R scripts and a browser.

Short instructions

The median instruction was 11 words long, and 73% were 25 words or fewer. Short instructions work only when the agent already knows the house rules, the projects and what each client expects. That knowledge sits in plain files the agent reads at the start of every session: rules, past decisions and the state of each workstream.

A lot of work behind each one

One instruction set off a median of 27 tool actions, 14 of them shell commands: reading and editing files, searching, running scripts and checking what came back. Every action is in the log, which is what made this page possible.

Time to done

Half of the instructions with tool work were done within 10.4 minutes, and the middle half took between 2.8 and 39 minutes. 57% were done within a quarter of an hour; 19% ran past an hour.

What the numbers say about rolling out an AI coding tool

1Watch the work one instruction carries

From the week of 5 October to the week of 26 October the median instruction got shorter, from 13.5 to 10 words, while the tool actions it set off rose from 10 to 35 and the median time to done went from 6 to 12 minutes. My reading: the house rules and past decisions had moved into the agent's files, so each instruction carried only what was new.

In a customer's team, shorter instructions doing more work is the sign that this groundwork has been done. A seat count never shows it.

  1. 2Put the repeatable parts in code the agent calls

    66% of instructions with tool work ran at least one R script. Fetching, counting, checking and rendering live in scripts; the agent decides which to run and reads what comes back. That keeps the work repeatable and easy to review. With a customer I would track the share of agent work that runs through their own scripts and CI checks, and push it up.

  2. 3Report the spread of time to done

    An average hides both ends. Here half the work was done within 10.4 minutes and 19% took more than an hour. The long tail is where review, tests and handover matter most, and where a rollout wins or loses the team's trust, so it gets its own line in every review.

  3. 4Cost is a context problem

    98.2% of input tokens were read from cache, which is what a stable context buys: the same rules and files, reused turn after turn. The input bill came to 13.4% of the uncached price. Teams that keep their repository rules and docs steady get the same effect, and cache share per team deserves a line in every account review.

  4. 5Agree the limits before asking anyone to change

    The agent never typed a client's password, and every message to a client went out only after a person had read it. For a bank or an insurer I would agree limits like these with their security team in the first week, before anyone is asked to change how they work.

The first 90 days at an Indian engineering organisation

Written for a typical account: an IT services firm, a global capability centre or a BFSI engineering team of a few hundred developers.

the metric is chosen with them on day one, never after

DaysWhat happensWhat gets measured
1 to 15 Pick two or three teams with a real backlog: a migration, a test gap, a slow review queue. Map how code moves from ticket to production. Agree one outcome metric with the engineering lead, and agree data and access limits with security: for banks and NBFCs, the RBI Directions on Outsourcing of Information Technology Services, 2023; for personal data, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and its Rules of 2025. A baseline from their own systems: cycle time, review wait, escaped defects or hours spent on the chosen backlog.
16 to 45 One champion for every ten engineers. Five starting use cases, each written up as a repeatable recipe: test generation, a migration path, a pull-request review loop, incident to fix, and getting a new joiner productive on a legacy module. Repository rules written with the team, not for them. Weekly office hours. Active users, instructions per developer per day, work per instruction, time to done, and the share of work that passes CI.
46 to 90 Take the recipes that worked to the next teams and retire the ones that did not. A readout to the CTO: the agreed metric before and after, cost per outcome, and the risks we closed. The agreed metric against its baseline, depth of use by team, cache share, and the plan for the next quarter.

Who wrote this

I am a doctor by training (MBBS, SMS Medical College, Jaipur) and the co-founder and COO of Scholar Scribe Consulting in New Delhi, where I run operations and client delivery. Since 2025 we have delivered research work to more than 100 paying clinicians on AI systems we built ourselves: the agent set-up measured here, an evidence-synthesis pipeline behind 60+ systematic reviews, a 135M-parameter clinical note reader delivered to a hepatology client, and MCP servers that bring our apps into AI assistants.

The part of the work I like most is the part this role is about: sitting with the people who use the system, finding the real bottleneck, shipping a fix in days and measuring whether it worked.

How the numbers were made. A script read every agent session log on our laptop for 3 October to 7 November 2026 and wrote out counts only, one row per instruction from a person: words, tool calls, shell commands, R script runs, and the time from the instruction to the agent's last action. Token counts come from the same logs, counted once per model message. No log text left the laptop. Work and time figures cover the 2,977 instructions that set off tool work.
